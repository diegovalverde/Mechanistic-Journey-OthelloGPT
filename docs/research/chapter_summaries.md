# Chapter Summaries

This internal authoring memory summarizes the narrative state of the book after the ten main chapters. It is meant for future maintenance, not as a substitute for the chapters or research memory.

## Chapter 1 - Can a Transformer Build a World Model?

### Core question

Can a Transformer trained only on Othello move sequences build an internal representation of the hidden board state, and what would count as evidence for that claim?

### Concepts introduced

- Othello move transcripts as visible sequences generated by a hidden board.
- World model as an internal representation of useful latent environment state.
- The distinction between behavior, representation, causal use, and mechanism.
- Othello-GPT as a small GPT-2-style decoder-only Transformer, not GPT-2 itself.
- The provenance rule: executed notebook to research memory to book prose.

### Equations / notation introduced

- Move prefix \(m_1, m_2, \ldots, m_t\).
- True board state \(B_t\).
- Next-move distribution \(p(m_{t+1} \mid m_{\le t})\).
- Move logit \(z(m \mid m_{\le t})\).
- Hidden activation \(h_t\).

### Experimental result

Chapter 1 previews the reproduced board-probe result but does not itself run a new experiment. It reports that later evidence shows a linear probe reached `0.9796` overall validation accuracy on held-out positions, with details deferred to Chapter 2.

### Evidence boundary

The chapter does not establish that Othello-GPT uses a board model. It only frames the investigation and separates decodability from causal use.

It also does not claim that the model represents the board in human form, stores a symbolic simulator, or computes legal moves by the same algorithm a hand-written Othello engine would use. The chapter's role is to make those later claims testable rather than assumed.

### Transition

The next chapter asks the first empirical question: can we decode the invisible board from an internal residual-stream activation?

## Chapter 2 - Learning to See the Invisible Board

### Core question

Can a simple readout recover the simulator-computed board state from Othello-GPT activations on held-out games?

### Concepts introduced

- Linear probes as external observational instruments.
- Relative square labels: empty, mine, theirs.
- Layer-4 residual stream at the final prefix token as the probe input.
- Strict game-level train/validation split and exact-prefix overlap removal.
- Probe-weight differences as candidate semantic directions.

### Equations / notation introduced

- Square state \(B_t(q)\).
- Activation \(h_t \in \mathbb{R}^{512}\).
- Probe output \(\text{probe}(h_t) \in \mathbb{R}^{64 \times 3}\).
- Probe logits \(s(q,c) = W_{q,c} \cdot h_t + b_{q,c}\).
- Mine-vs-theirs direction \(v_{q,\text{mine-vs-theirs}} = W_{q,\text{mine}} - W_{q,\text{theirs}}\).
- Occupied-vs-empty direction from average occupied weights minus empty weight.

### Experimental result

The executed notebook trained a layer-4 linear board probe on `blocks.4.hook_resid_post[:, -1, :]`. With 120 training games and 30 validation games, it collected 1,318 train positions and 330 validation positions after removing 2 overlapping train prefixes. Overall validation accuracy was `0.9796`; empty, mine, and theirs accuracies were `0.9976`, `0.9561`, and `0.9703`. Per-square accuracy ranged from `0.9364` to `0.9970`.

### Evidence boundary

The result establishes high linear decodability of board state from layer-4 activations under this split. It does not establish causal use, component identity, a symbolic board variable, or a legality circuit.

### Transition

Chapter 3 asks why a successful probe is not enough and introduces residual-space intervention as the next evidence rung.

## Chapter 3 - Why Probing Isn't Enough

### Core question

If the board is decodable, does that mean the model uses the decoded board representation when predicting moves?

### Concepts introduced

- Probes as observational instruments rather than model components.
- Decodability versus causal use.
- Correlated-passenger, redundant-representation, and misaligned-readout failure modes.
- Semantic residual-space interventions.
- Off-manifold risk and the need for small edits and controls.

### Equations / notation introduced

- Semantic residual edit \(h' = h + \alpha v\).
- Logit change \(\Delta z_m = z_m(h + \alpha v) - z_m(h)\).

### Experimental result

No new standalone experiment is introduced in this chapter. It interprets the probe and previews that executed semantic residual interventions support local causal relevance in tested cases.

### Evidence boundary

The chapter does not prove causal use from probe accuracy. It also does not claim that a residual edit literally changes a board variable. The semantic direction remains an operational probe-derived handle that may mix correlated features.

It further keeps negative intervention interpretations open: if a chosen probe direction failed to affect behavior, that would not by itself prove board information is unused. The direction might be redundant, misaligned with the causal basis, too weak, or inserted at the wrong site.

The chapter therefore turns the probe result into a handle rather than a conclusion. It explains why small, sign-controlled residual edits, unrelated-square controls, and later component/path analyses are needed before the book can move from representation claims to mechanism claims.

### Transition

The chapter ends by asking whether we can predict the effect of small internal nudges in general. Chapter 4 answers with local derivatives and Jacobians.

## Chapter 4 - Jacobians: Listening to the Network's Thoughts

### Core question

Can a local Jacobian quantitatively predict the effect of small residual-stream interventions on Othello-GPT logits?

### Concepts introduced

- Derivatives as local slopes.
- Gradients for one output logit with respect to a residual activation.
- Jacobians for many output logits with respect to residual coordinates.
- Jacobian-vector products as predicted output changes for chosen directions.
- Hook-defined downstream functions in TransformerLens.
- Finite-difference validation.

### Equations / notation introduced

- Scalar approximation \(z(x+\delta x) \approx z(x) + \frac{dz}{dx}\delta x\).
- Gradient \(\nabla_h z_m\).
- Directional derivative \(\delta z_m \approx \alpha \nabla_h z_m^\top v\).
- Logit Jacobian \(J(h) = \partial z / \partial h\), shape `[61, 512]`.
- Vector approximation \(z(h+\delta h) \approx z(h) + J(h)\delta h\).

### Experimental result

At `blocks.4.hook_resid_post`, final prefix position 27, the notebook first validated the selected E3 logit gradient along the normalized gradient direction for epsilons from `0.0001` to `0.1`. It then used the normalized G6 mine-vs-theirs probe direction, whose directional derivative for the E3 logit was `+0.030897`. Across alphas from `-0.1` to `0.1`, predicted and actual logit deltas matched with maximum absolute error `0.000067`.

### Evidence boundary

This is strong evidence for local first-order prediction in the tested setup. It does not show global linearity, generalize automatically to all positions or directions, or identify the components that transmit the effect.

### Transition

Chapter 5 asks how the same semantic direction is transformed into later hidden representations across different contexts.

## Chapter 5 - J-Space

### Core question

How does downstream computation transform an interpretable source direction, and how context dependent is that transformation?

### Concepts introduced

- A Jacobian as a point-dependent local map.
- Hidden-state transport rather than logit-space sensitivity.
- Local J-space direction \(J_x v\).
- Average transformed direction \(\mathbb{E}_x[J_x v]\).
- Cosine similarity as an angle-based comparison, not a percentage.
- Relationship to the broader Jacobian Lens and J-space literature.

### Equations / notation introduced

- Context-dependent Jacobian \(J_x = \partial F_x / \partial h_4\).
- Local transformed direction \(v'_x = J_x v\).
- Average transformed direction \(\bar{v}_J = \frac{1}{N}\sum_i J_{x_i}v\).

### Experimental result

The source was the normalized G6 mine-vs-theirs direction at `blocks.4.hook_resid_post`, final prefix position 27. The target was the final residual stream at the same token position, before final normalization and unembedding. The local JVP finite-difference validation reported cosine `0.999944` and relative error `0.010651`. Averaging \(J_i v\) over 100 independently sampled Othello positions gave local-vs-average cosine `0.617840`; `||J_local_v||` was `1.496970`, `||J_avg_v||` was `0.819020`, and the final-readout effect of the averaged direction was `0.018023` versus `0.030897` locally.

### Evidence boundary

The result is moderate evidence for shared transformed geometry plus context-dependent variation for one direction and setup. It does not characterize every semantic direction, every layer, or the full legality computation.

### Transition

Chapter 6 opens the downstream Transformer architecture to identify where such transformations could be carried by residual streams, attention, MLPs, and hookable components.

## Chapter 6 - Information Flow Through a Transformer

### Core question

Which operations inside the Transformer can retrieve, combine, transform, and write information as semantic directions move downstream?

### Concepts introduced

- Residual stream as the shared communication space.
- Zero-based layer numbering for the eight-block Othello-GPT model.
- Attention as cross-position routing and writing.
- MLP as position-wise nonlinear transformation after attention has updated the current residual row.
- Neuron read-gate-write framing.
- TransformerLens hook points.
- Attribution versus ablation as distinct evidence types.

### Equations / notation introduced

- Block equations: \(a_l = \mathrm{Attention}_l(\mathrm{Norm}_1(r_l))\), \(r_l^\text{mid}=r_l+a_l\), \(m_l=\mathrm{MLP}_l(\mathrm{Norm}_2(r_l^\text{mid}))\), \(r_{l+1}=r_l^\text{mid}+m_l\).
- Schematic residual sum \(r_\text{final}=r_\text{initial}+\sum_l a_l+\sum_l m_l\).
- MLP neuron contribution \(g_j W_\text{out}[j,:]\).
- Component attribution \(g^\top c\).

### Experimental result

No new Othello result is claimed. The chapter records verified architecture facts: `n_layers=8`, `d_model=512`, `n_heads=8`, `d_head=64`, `d_mlp=2048`, `d_vocab=61`, `n_ctx=59`, GELU MLPs, and `LNPre` normalization. It defines hook names used later.

### Evidence boundary

The chapter is architectural and methodological. Attention patterns are explicitly treated as hypothesis-generating evidence, not circuits. Attribution is not described as ablation.

It also does not rank any layer, head, MLP, or neuron. The architecture explains where measurements can attach and why the same residual-space write can be compared to a downstream gradient, but it does not supply evidence that any component implements the Othello rule.

### Transition

Chapter 7 uses these instruments to ask where board-state geometry becomes especially aligned with legal-move computation.

## Chapter 7 - The Mystery of Layer 7

### Core question

Where in the eight-block stack does board-state sensitivity become selectively aligned with the capture-line structure that makes a move legal?

### Concepts introduced

- Legality contrast as selected legal move logit minus mean currently illegal empty-square move logit.
- Illegal empty-square baseline excluding occupied squares, starting-center non-move squares, and pass.
- Capture-line opponent and friendly-terminator roles.
- Layer sweep over semantic direction sensitivities.
- Bootstrap over positions and shuffled-square controls.

### Equations / notation introduced

- Legality contrast \(L_m^{\text{legality}} = z_m - \operatorname{mean}_{j \in I} z_j\).
- Legal-preference contrast \(L_m^{\text{legal-pref}} = z_m - \operatorname{mean}_{j \in \mathcal{L}\setminus\{m\}} z_j\).
- Legality gradient \(g_m^\text{legality} = \nabla_{h_{\ell,t}} L_m^\text{legality}\).
- Square sensitivity \(S_{\ell,q} = v_{q,\text{mine-vs-theirs}}^\top g_m^\text{legality}\).
- Layer ratio \(R_\ell\) comparing capture support to unrelated occupied controls.

### Experimental result

For the concrete E3 example, raw E3 logit was `8.9408`, mean illegal empty-square logit `-1.5438`, and legality contrast `10.4845`. The first layer-4 aggregate was weak: mean(capture - unrelated occupied) `-0.000499`, bootstrap CI `[-0.003646, 0.002784]`. The layer sweep over layers 2, 4, 6, and 7 found layer-7 capture-vs-unrelated ratio `2.251362` and capture-minus-unrelated `0.026569`, while other tested layers were near one. Validation focused on layer-7 capture opponents: capture mean `0.063157`, unrelated occupied mean `0.022995`, ratio `2.746573`, difference `0.040162`, ratio CI `[2.524081, 2.971348]`, shuffled mean ratio `1.046078`, shuffled 95th percentile `1.176336`, empirical p-value `0.003322`.

### Evidence boundary

The chapter supports layer 7 as the strongest tested site for capture-line legality enrichment. It does not identify a responsible component, a path, sign-resolved rule computation, or a complete legality algorithm.

### Transition

Chapter 8 opens layer 7 and asks which components inside the final block matter most for the legality contrast.

## Chapter 8 - MLP7

### Core question

Within layer 7, which component most strongly contributes to the selected-move legality contrast, and is the effect concentrated in a candidate neuron population?

### Concepts introduced

- Layer-7 component list: L7H0 through L7H7 and MLP7.
- Signed and absolute component attribution.
- Mean-replacement component ablation and its sign convention.
- Attention-to-MLP7 hypothesis: MLP7 reads `resid_mid`, after layer-7 attention.
- MLP7 neuron decomposition through post-GELU activation times output vector.
- Candidate neuron ranking and top-N group ablation.

### Equations / notation introduced

- Legality contrast \(L_m = z_m - \operatorname{mean}_{j \in I} z_j\).
- Component attribution \(A_c = g^\top c\).
- Component-ablation effect \(\Delta L_c = L_m(\text{ablate } c) - L_m(\text{clean})\).
- MLP neuron contribution \(c_j = g_j W_\text{out}[j,:]\).
- Neuron-group `legality_degradation` \(D_N = L_\text{clean} - L_\text{ablated}\).

### Experimental result

In the E3 example, MLP7 had absolute attribution `0.560007`, largest among layer-7 components. Across 30 positions, MLP7 had mean absolute attribution `0.267666`, ahead of L7H0 `0.201140`, L7H2 `0.186625`, and L7H7 `0.180907`. Component ablation also ranked MLP7 first with mean signed effect `-0.105164` and mean absolute effect `0.262614`; the next largest individual head was L7H7 at `0.109719`. The top-20 fixed candidate neurons were selected by mean absolute MLP7 attribution, led by neuron 399 with mean absolute attribution `0.276027`. Top-N neuron-group ablations separated strongly from random same-size groups; top-20 mean was `-0.543530` versus random mean `0.012949` under the `L_clean - L_ablate` convention.

### Evidence boundary

The chapter supports MLP7 component importance and candidate-neuron concentration. It does not show that MLP7 computes the Othello rule, that attention is irrelevant, or that any candidate neuron is a rule neuron.

### Transition

## Chapter 9 - Hunting Rule Circuits

### Core question

Do the fixed high-attribution MLP7 candidate neurons implement recognizable pieces of the Othello capture rule?

### Concepts introduced

- Localization is not interpretation.
- Rule-neuron hypothesis as a falsifiable target.
- Relational-condition datasets.
- Matched valid-vs-invalid controls.
- Detector versus writer distinction.
- Input-weight geometry versus output-weight geometry.
- Additive versus interaction regression.
- Rescue as an absent but desired sufficiency-like test.
- Distributed MLP7 population hypothesis.

### Equations / notation introduced

- Standardized selectivity \(S_j\) comparing valid captures with opponent-without-terminator examples.
- Matched pair activation difference \(\Delta a_j = a_j(\text{valid}) - a_j(\text{matched invalid})\).
- Interaction-regression comparison between additive features and opponent-adjacent by friendly-terminator interactions.
- Activation-by-legality-write score using mean activation times mean output-vector dot legality gradient.

### Experimental result

The relational-condition dataset had `763` valid-capture, `515` multiple-capture, `654` opponent-without-terminator, `563` friendly-adjacent, and `467` empty-adjacent examples. Unpaired valid-vs-invalid selectivity was small and mixed: neuron 1167 was `0.107989`, 734 `0.094814`, 1747 `0.081680`, while neuron 399 was `-0.125210`. Across 654 matched pairs, the largest mean valid-minus-invalid values were 991 `0.069904`, 877 `0.053469`, 272 `0.047312`, and 1167 `0.039280`; medians were `0.0` for all candidates, and neuron 399 remained negative at `-0.089261`. Line-structure correlations were small over 1,278 valid-condition examples. Interaction-regression delta \(R^2\) was at most `0.002291`. Input-weight cosines were small. Output-side geometry was more interpretable for neuron 399, whose mean post-activation was `0.604659`, mean legality-gradient dot `-0.127217`, and activation-by-legality-write `-0.076923`. The combined-evidence top-five neurons averaged `-0.019919` legality degradation over 195 examples, compared with controls near zero.

### Evidence boundary

The chapter does not establish a monosemantic rule neuron, a full attention-to-MLP-to-logit circuit, path mediation, rescue, or a complete algorithmic explanation. It supports a distributed MLP7 population hypothesis.

### Transition

## Chapter 10 - Beyond Othello

### Core question

Which parts of the Othello-GPT investigation were specific to a tiny board game, and which form a general methodology for mechanistic interpretability?

### Concepts introduced

- Evidence ladder as a working discipline rather than a universal taxonomy.
- Ground-truth spectrum from formal synthetic worlds to open-ended language.
- Latent-variable problem outside domains with simulators.
- The claim that mathematics generalizes more easily than semantics.
- Portable roles: read, route, transform, write, and population behavior.
- Map versus sensitivity map versus localization versus mechanism.
- Future programs for Othello and larger models.

### Equations / notation introduced

- Reuses \(B_t\), \(h' = h + \alpha v\), \(J(h)\delta h\), \(g^\top c\), and \(J_x v\) as durable methodology notation.

### Experimental result

No new experiment; conceptual synthesis chapter. It creates conceptual figures for the mechanistic workflow, why Othello is special, the ground-truth spectrum, mechanistic roles, Othello next experiments, and map-to-mechanism hierarchy.

### Evidence boundary

The chapter explicitly does not claim a completed Othello legality circuit, a rule neuron, rescue, sufficiency, or new experimental results in language models, robotics, code models, or other domains. Outside-Othello examples are proposed applications or thought experiments unless cited as prior work.

It treats superposition only as a possible explanatory frame for messy neuron-level results, not as an experimentally established explanation of this model.

### Transition

This is the final narrative chapter. It leaves the book with a research program: build mechanistic claims through evidence gates, preserve negative results, and reduce claim strength when moving away from domains with known ground-truth latent state.
