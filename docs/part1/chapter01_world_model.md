# Can a Transformer Build a World Model?

Imagine that you have never seen an Othello board.

No diagram. No rulebook. No explanation that the game is played on an 8 by 8 grid. No idea that pieces flip from one color to another. You are only handed transcripts:

```text
C4 C3 D3 ...
```

Then another:

```text
F5 F6 E6 ...
```

Then thousands more. Then millions.

Each transcript is just a sequence of symbols. A symbol looks like a coordinate, but at first that does not help much. You do not know what the coordinates mean. You do not know why some coordinates appear early and others never appear until later. You do not know why the four central squares are absent as opening moves. You do not know why some moves become possible only after a particular pattern of earlier moves.

Now someone asks you to play the same game:

```text
C4 C3 D3 E3 B2 ...
```

What move comes next?

One possible strategy is memorization. If the exact prefix has appeared before, repeat whatever came next in the training data. If a similar prefix appeared, imitate it. If certain pairs or triples of coordinates tend to occur together, learn those surface statistics. This will work sometimes. Many sequence tasks reward pattern matching, and a sufficiently large table of common fragments can look surprisingly competent.

But the transcripts have a hidden cause. They were not generated by a bag of coordinate n-grams. They were generated by a changing board.

After each move, a physical-looking state changes: one square becomes occupied, some existing pieces may flip, the player to move changes, and the set of legal next moves changes with it. The sequence is visible. The board is latent. If you want to predict future moves well, it may become useful to reconstruct some version of that latent board from the move history.

This is the first mystery of the book. A Transformer trained only on move sequences has no board drawn on its input. It receives tokens and is rewarded for predicting the next token. Yet prior work, and our own reproduction, find evidence that board-state information becomes available inside the model. The question is not merely whether the model gets good at the task. The question is what kind of internal machinery makes that competence possible.

    If a model can predict legal Othello moves from move history alone, what are the simplest strategies it might use? Which of those strategies would require tracking the board?

## The Board Behind the Sequence

Othello, also called Reversi, is a two-player game played on an 8 by 8 board. Each square is either empty or contains a disc belonging to one of the two players. The game begins with four discs in the center. Players take turns placing a disc of their own color on an empty square.

The important rule is not just "place a disc." A move is legal only if the new disc brackets at least one contiguous line of opponent discs, with one of your own discs at the far end.

In one direction, the pattern looks like this:

```text
target empty square
    opponent disc
    opponent disc
    your disc
```

If you place your disc on the target square, the opponent discs in between flip to your color. The same idea applies horizontally, vertically, and diagonally. A single move can flip discs along more than one direction.

This rule is local enough to state in a sentence, but its consequences are global enough to make the game interesting. A move played ten turns ago can change which discs exist now. A disc that was yours can become your opponent's. A square that was illegal can become legal after a distant-looking flip. The current legal moves are not written directly in the transcript. They have to be inferred from the state produced by all earlier moves.

That is why Othello is a useful laboratory. The input is a sequence, like text. But unlike text, the hidden state that generated the sequence is exactly recoverable. If we have the move history and the rules, we can replay the game and know the board after every move.

This gives us two perspectives on the same situation. From outside the model, we can compute the true board. From inside the model, there are only vectors. Mechanistic interpretability begins when we ask whether those vectors contain a recognizable version of the hidden state, and whether the network uses it in producing its output.

For now, keep the capture pattern in mind: target empty, opponent line, friendly terminator. We will return to it again and again. It is the seed of a much deeper question. Does the model merely learn that some coordinate strings tend to follow other coordinate strings, or does it build internal variables corresponding to board facts that make moves legal?

We should be careful with the word "build." We are not claiming that the Transformer writes a symbolic Othello engine in Python inside its weights. We are not claiming that it represents the board the way a human player imagines a board. We are asking a more operational question: is information about the latent board present in the model's activations, and can that information help explain its next-move predictions?

## A Language Model, but With Coordinates

The reason Othello-GPT is so tempting to study is that it has the same outer shape as a language model.

A language model receives tokens so far and produces a probability distribution over the next token:

```text
tokens so far
    -> hidden computation
    -> distribution over next token
```

Othello-GPT receives moves so far and produces a probability distribution over the next move:

```text
moves so far
    -> hidden computation
    -> distribution over next move
```

The similarity is structural, not semantic. Othello-GPT is not GPT-2 itself. It is a small GPT-2-style decoder-only Transformer trained from scratch on Othello move sequences. Its tokens are board coordinates, plus any special tokens used by the training setup. Its training objective is next-move prediction. It is not pretrained on internet text, and it is not given a rendered board as input.

This setup removes many distractions that make ordinary language models hard to reason about.

In natural language, the hidden state behind a conversation is broad and partly unknowable. If someone writes "the cup fell because it was near the edge," the true world includes objects, gravity, intentions, camera angles, missing context, and common-sense assumptions. We do not have a simulator that tells us the exact latent state of the conversation after each word. We can annotate, approximate, and argue, but there is no unique ground-truth world state.

In Othello, we know every square.

After a move sequence, we can compute which squares are empty, which contain the current player's discs, which contain the opponent's discs, which moves are legal, which rays would capture, and which pieces would flip. The game gives us a precise external answer key for a latent-state question.

That answer key is what turns Othello-GPT from a cute toy into a serious scientific instrument. It lets us ask questions about internal representation with a kind of precision that is rarely available in language. When we look at an activation vector and ask whether it contains the board, we are not guessing what the board "should" be. We can calculate it.

## What Counts as a World Model?

"World model" is an overloaded phrase. It can sound philosophical, and in larger AI debates it often carries more weight than any single experiment can support. In this book we will use a deliberately modest definition.

A world model is an internal representation that tracks aspects of a latent environment useful for predicting its evolution or choosing appropriate actions.

This definition has three important parts.

First, the representation is internal. It lives in the model's activations or weights, not in the external simulator we use as researchers. If we replay the moves ourselves and compute the board, that is our board model, not necessarily the Transformer's.

Second, it tracks aspects of a latent environment. The model does not have to encode every detail. A chess-playing model might track piece locations but not the color of the table. An Othello sequence model might track current-player occupancy and opponent occupancy because those facts matter for move prediction.

Third, the representation is useful for prediction or action. A random encoding of the board that accidentally exists in a corner of activation space would be less interesting than a representation that participates in the computation leading to the next move.

This last point creates the main tension of Part I.

There is a difference between saying:

```text
The model behaves as if it knows the board.
```

and saying:

```text
We have evidence that the board is represented internally.
```

There is another difference between saying:

```text
The board is represented internally.
```

and saying:

```text
The model uses that representation when choosing moves.
```

These sentences are easy to blur together. A major purpose of the book is to keep them separate.

If a model usually predicts legal moves, it behaves as if it has access to legal-move information. That behavior is the mystery, not the explanation. If a probe can decode the board from an activation, that tells us board information is available somewhere in the network. That is stronger, but still not the whole explanation. To show use, we need causal evidence: when we change or remove the representation in a controlled way, the model's output should change in a way that matches the role we claim the representation plays.

Mechanistic interpretability lives in these distinctions. It is not satisfied with "the model works." It asks what variables are represented, where they are represented, how they are transformed, and whether those transformations are part of the computation.

## Just Enough Notation

Let us put names on the pieces of the puzzle.

Write the observed move sequence as:

$$
m_1, m_2, \ldots, m_t
$$

Here \(m_1\) is the first move, \(m_2\) is the second move, and \(m_t\) is the most recent move we have observed. In the transcript:

```text
C4 C3 D3
```

we could say \(m_1 = C4\), \(m_2 = C3\), and \(m_3 = D3\).

Now let:

$$
B_t
$$

be the true Othello board after those \(t\) moves have been played. This is not given to the model as a picture. It is the board we can compute by applying the rules.

The model's job is to predict the next move. In probability notation, it estimates:

$$
p(m_{t+1} \mid m_1, m_2, \ldots, m_t)
$$

Read this as: a distribution over the next move, given the moves so far.

Transformers usually produce unnormalized scores called logits before those scores are turned into probabilities. We can write the logit for a candidate move \(m\) as:

$$
z(m \mid m_{\le t})
$$

The shorthand \(m_{\le t}\) just means "all moves up to time \(t\)." A higher logit generally means the model is assigning that move a higher probability after the softmax.

The world-model hypothesis says that somewhere in the hidden computation there may be a vector:

$$
h_t
$$

that carries information about \(B_t\). Conceptually:

```text
m_{\le t}
    -> h_t
    -> next-move prediction
```

This diagram is not proof. It is a research question in diagram form. The vector \(h_t\) may contain board information. It may contain other information. Board information may be spread across many vectors and layers rather than living in one neat place. The model may also use a representation that is not aligned with the categories we would naturally choose.

Still, the notation helps us state the core possibility:

$$
m_{\le t} \text{ determines } B_t,
$$

and the model may learn internal features that track \(B_t\) because those features are useful for predicting \(m_{t+1}\).

The rest of the investigation asks how much of that sentence is true, in what sense, and with what evidence.

## Why Othello Is a Clean Test

The cleanest experiments are often built from artificial constraints. Othello-GPT is useful not because Othello is the most important task in AI, but because the task creates an unusually clear separation between what the model sees and what the researcher knows.

The model sees a prefix of moves. We know the board.

The model predicts a distribution over coordinates. We know which coordinates are legal.

The model stores intermediate results in high-dimensional vectors. We know many candidate facts those vectors might represent: whether A1 is empty, whether C4 belongs to the current player, whether F6 is an opponent disc, whether a candidate target has an opponent line in a diagonal direction, whether a friendly disc terminates that line.

That makes Othello a bridge between toy worlds and real models. On one side, it is small enough that we can name the relevant latent variables. On the other side, it is still a Transformer doing sequence prediction with distributed activations, attention, nonlinearities, and all the usual interpretability headaches. It is not a hand-written program with variables named `board` and `legal_moves`. If a board is inside the model, it is encoded in learned vector geometry.

This matters because many claims about neural networks have the following shape:

```text
The model must know X, because it can do Y.
```

Sometimes that inference is reasonable. Sometimes it is a trap. A model can exploit shortcuts, dataset regularities, position-specific correlations, or memorized fragments. It can solve a training objective in a way that is not the way we would solve it.

Othello lets us push past vague arguments. We can build datasets of positions. We can compute exact board labels. We can train probes to decode those labels from activations. We can test held-out games rather than only familiar prefixes. Later, we can intervene on hidden directions and ask whether logits move as predicted.

The phrase "ground truth" is overused, but here it earns its place. For each moment in a game, we can compute the true board state. We can compute legal moves. We can compute the capture rays that make those moves legal. That gives us ground truth for the model's possible internal world.

In language, if an activation appears to encode "the speaker's intention," the target itself is uncertain. In Othello, if an activation appears to encode "the square D3 is occupied by the current player's disc," the target is exact.

This does not make interpretation easy. It makes it falsifiable.

## The Original Result

Othello-GPT entered the interpretability conversation through the Othello World work by Li et al., published as *Emergent World Representations: Exploring a Sequence Model Trained on a Synthetic Task*. Their setup trained a GPT-style sequence model to predict legal moves in Othello from move transcripts. The striking claim was not merely that the model became good at next-move prediction. It was that internal activations carried evidence of an emergent board-state representation.

Neel Nanda later revisited the model in TransformerLens, a library designed for inspecting GPT-style models through cached activations, hooks, and interventions. That follow-up helped make Othello-GPT a practical playground for mechanistic interpretability: small enough to run and probe, but rich enough that the answers are not trivial.

Our project begins from that lineage and then keeps a research memory of what has actually been reproduced or tested. The rule for this book is strict: scientific claims flow from executed notebooks into the research log, then into the findings snapshot and experiment index, and only then into polished prose.

At the time of this chapter, our own strict-split reproduction supports the claim that board state is represented in the residual stream. A linear probe reached 0.9796 overall validation accuracy over 330 held-out validation positions, with per-class accuracies 0.9976 for empty squares, 0.9561 for current-player squares, and 0.9703 for opponent squares. Those details belong properly to Chapter 2, where we will unpack the data split, the probe, the labels, and the failure modes.

For Chapter 1, the result plays a simpler role. It tells us that the opening mystery is not imaginary. In a real trained Othello-GPT model, board-state information is not just a convenient story we tell from outside. Under a controlled reproduction, it is decodable from internal activations with high held-out accuracy.

But the next sentence is just as important:

Decodable does not mean causally used.

## The Philosophical Trap

Suppose we pause a Transformer after a move sequence and extract one of its activation vectors. We train a separate classifier, outside the model, to read the board from that vector. The classifier performs extremely well.

What have we learned?

We have learned that the information is available in the activation. That is a real discovery. If the board could not be recovered from the vector, then a simple board-representation story would be much less plausible.

But availability is not use.

Imagine a phone number written in a notebook on someone's desk. The number is available in the room. If the person later makes a phone call, we cannot conclude from availability alone that they consulted the notebook. They might have remembered the number. They might have called someone else. They might have used a speed-dial entry. The notebook matters only if it participates in the causal chain that produced the call.

The same caution applies to probes. A probe is an observer we train after the fact. It can discover information in the model's activations that the model itself may or may not rely on. Neural networks often carry side information. A feature can be correlated with the real cause without being the cause. A representation can be readable from a vector without being read by later layers.

This is the central trap:

```text
decodable information != causally used information
```

Chapter 2 will ask whether we can find the board. Chapter 3 will ask why finding the board is not enough. Later chapters will introduce tools for asking causal questions: what happens if we nudge a semantic direction, remove a component, compare a local linear prediction to an actual intervention, or trace how a direction is transformed across the network?

This is where mechanistic interpretability becomes more than visualization. A visualization can suggest a story. A probe can reveal available information. A causal intervention can test whether the story has teeth.

Even then, caution remains. A local intervention can show that changing a direction changes a logit in a predicted way. That is stronger than decodability, but it is not automatically a complete circuit. A component ablation can show that a part of the model matters, but not exactly what algorithm it implements. A neuron can have a selective effect without being a clean symbolic variable.

This book will keep those distinctions visible. The aim is not to make the strongest possible claim at every step. The aim is to build claims that can survive contact with the evidence.

## The Road Ahead

The investigation will proceed by turning a vague question into sharper ones.

First: can we find the board? We will train simple readout models on internal activations and ask whether they recover the true Othello board on held-out games. This is the first contact between the model's hidden vectors and the simulator's exact state.

Second: can we identify semantic directions? If a probe distinguishes "mine" from "theirs" on a square, does that give us a meaningful direction in activation space? Can we treat some directions as corresponding to board facts, at least operationally?

Third: if we perturb those directions, do outputs change? This moves from reading to intervening. The question becomes not just whether information is present, but whether small controlled changes to that information move next-move logits in the expected direction.

Fourth: how are semantic directions transformed across the model? A Transformer has layers. Information can be copied, rotated, amplified, suppressed, or recombined. We will need tools for asking how a meaningful direction at one point in the network affects a meaningful direction later.

Fifth: where does representation become rule-sensitive computation? Othello legality is not just a list of occupied squares. It depends on relationships among squares: empty target, opponent line, friendly terminator. At some point, board facts have to become move-relevant evidence.

Sixth: can that computation be localized? We will descend from broad representations to layers, components, and eventually candidate neurons. This does not mean we expect one neuron to contain the whole rule. It means we will ask how much of the computation can be assigned to identifiable parts of the network, and how much remains distributed.

Seventh: can we describe a candidate circuit? A circuit claim should say more than "this part matters." It should describe an algorithmic pathway: what information enters, how it is transformed, and how it changes the output. The current research record does not establish a complete legality circuit, so this remains a destination rather than a premise.

The purpose of this roadmap is to make the later mathematics feel inevitable. Linear probes, residual streams, Jacobians, intervention tests, and component decompositions are not techniques for their own sake. Each one answers a pressure created by the previous question.

If the board is not decodable, the world-model story weakens. If it is decodable but interventions do nothing, the causal story weakens. If interventions work locally but cannot be traced through the network, the mechanistic story remains incomplete. If a component matters but no algorithmic account survives controls, then we have found an important part without yet understanding it.

That is the journey: from behavior, to representation, to causal use, to mechanism.

## What This Chapter Establishes

We have not yet proven that Othello-GPT uses a board model. We have set up the question precisely enough to investigate it.

The model is trained on move sequences. The true board is hidden from the input but exactly computable by the researcher. Legal moves depend on relational board structure, especially the capture pattern of an empty target, an opponent line, and a friendly terminator. A world-model claim, in this book, means an operational claim about internal representations that track useful latent state. And any serious version of that claim must separate behavior, decodability, causal use, and full mechanism.

The next chapter begins with the first empirical step: looking inside the model's activations for the invisible board.
